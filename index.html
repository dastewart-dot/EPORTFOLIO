REFLECTIONS

How my producing practice developed.

1. Finding My Producer Voice

At the beginning of the program, I thought of producing mainly as the work required to organize a project and get it made. Through development, pitching and production exercises, I learned that a producer also has a creative voice. That voice appears in the projects I choose, the questions I ask, the resources I prioritize and the way I communicate a vision to other people.

This changed the way I approach my own material. I now look beyond whether an idea interests me and ask whether I understand its audience, scope, production needs and long-term potential. I have become more intentional about balancing the creative side of a project with the practical decisions necessary to move it forward.

2. Collaboration and Communication

Production work reinforced that a good idea cannot carry a project by itself. Every department needs information, expectations and a shared understanding of what the production is trying to accomplish. Collaboration taught me to communicate earlier, listen more carefully and make sure responsibilities are clear.

That lesson has made me a stronger producer because I now see communication as part of the creative process rather than an administrative task. When people understand the plan, they can contribute more confidently, problems are easier to identify and the production has a better chance of protecting the original creative intention.

3. From Idea to Production Package

Developing One Way Route pushed me to think beyond the concept itself. The budget, pitch, storyboards and other planning materials forced me to examine how the story would actually be produced. Each document answered a different production question and made the project more concrete.

I learned that a strong package creates confidence. It demonstrates that the producer understands both the story and the work required to execute it. That experience strengthened my ability to evaluate scope, anticipate production needs and communicate a project to collaborators or potential supporters.

4. Building a Television World

Full Circle taught me that television development requires a different kind of planning from a single short film. Characters need enough depth to sustain multiple episodes, conflicts need room to grow and the world must continually generate new story possibilities.

Working on the series bible and pilot helped me understand how character, theme and episode structure support one another. I became more conscious of the audience experience: what information to reveal, what questions to leave open and how the pilot establishes expectations for the series that follows.

5. Research as a Producing Tool

Research Methodologies expanded my understanding of how evidence can support producing decisions. Quantitative research can reveal patterns, audience behavior or market information, while qualitative research can provide context and explain why people respond the way they do.

As a producer, I can use both approaches when researching audiences, comparable projects, trends or the real-world subjects behind a story. The course encouraged me to question assumptions and make decisions from stronger information instead of relying only on instinct.

6. Business Behind the Creative Work

Courses involving finance, law, marketing and entrepreneurship showed me that producing continues far beyond development and the set. A project also exists within contracts, budgets, financing structures, distribution strategies and an increasingly competitive marketplace.

Building Stew Creative Productions helped connect those lessons. I began thinking about how individual projects can fit within a larger company identity and how a producer can build relationships, services and intellectual property over time. This gave me a more complete view of producing as both a creative profession and a business.

7. Curation and Professional Presentation

Curation taught me that presentation is itself a form of communication. A portfolio should not contain everything I have made. It should guide the viewer toward work that demonstrates specific skills, development and professional direction.

Revising this portfolio made that lesson practical. I had to separate the explanation of why I selected an artifact from the information a professional visitor actually needs: what the project is, what I did, what course or assignment produced it and what the work demonstrates about my abilities.

8. Moving Forward

The program gave me a broader understanding of the producer's responsibility from idea generation through development, production, business planning and presentation. I leave with stronger tools, but also with a clearer understanding that professional growth depends on continuing to use those tools outside the classroom.

My next step is to keep developing original work, strengthen Stew Creative Productions and continue building relationships with collaborators who can help move projects toward production and distribution. The portfolio represents completed coursework, but I want the projects themselves to continue developing beyond the program.
